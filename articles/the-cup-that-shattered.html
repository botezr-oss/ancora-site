The Cup That Shattered

When authenticity isn't weakness — it's emotional education, and it's connection.

I washed a cup and slammed it down on the drying rack so hard it shattered. My daughter looked at me with frightened eyes — not scared of the cup. Scared of me.

For two years, I played the role of the perfect mother: always calm, always balanced, always in control. I wasn't allowed to be angry. I wasn't allowed to be overwhelmed — because any "negative emotion" would traumatize the child, or so I believed.

But that day, I understood something uncomfortable: I wasn't protecting her. I was teaching her that emotions need to be hidden. I was showing her an impossible standard — a myth she would inevitably try to copy. So I started doing it differently. I began telling her:

"I'm sad, and that sadness is mine, not yours." "I'm angry, and that's my anger — you're not responsible for it." "I need five minutes to breathe and calm down."

Not to unload on her — but so she could see that emotions aren't dangerous, that they can be named, and naming them helps us manage them, because we understand what message they're carrying.

I grew up believing multitasking was a superpower. In reality, it's a direct road to burnout and self-erasure. Authenticity isn't weakness — it's emotional education, and it's connection.
