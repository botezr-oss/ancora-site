What Storming Taught Me About Leading Through Difference

How Tuckman's stages of team development shifted my understanding of conflict and human performance.

I became a team leader in 2015. In my early leadership training, we talked a lot about team formation — I first heard of Tuckman's model there: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning. For a long time, I shaped my leadership style around understanding those stages. But the stage that was hardest to manage, by far, was Storming.

I had colleagues who didn't just fail to collaborate — they didn't even want to speak to one another. Different personalities, different needs, different expectations. As a leader, it stopped being about tasks. It became about tension and emotional regulation. One-on-ones, aligning expectations, clarifying what we actually needed from each other — not to become friends, but to be able to work together — were what moved the team forward. That's really the whole point of Storming: finding a way to perform despite the differences.

I tested a lot of models trying to understand that dynamic — from Jung and Freud to Brené Brown, books on emotion and relationships, various business frameworks, and eventually coaching. The insight that stuck: we don't react to the present, we react to our past experiences.

Today, the environment has changed. It's no longer just about the interactions between people. As a leader, you're not just managing relationships anymore — you're also managing how people relate to technology. AI adds a new layer of complexity on top of something that was already hard. But one thing stays constant: human interaction. AI is a tool. Performance still comes from how people work together. A high-performing team doesn't just deliver when it can — it delivers better and faster, because the people in it know how to work together despite their differences.
