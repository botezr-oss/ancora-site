## Leadership & Teams

### Psychological Safety Isn't for Everyone

Amy Edmondson defined it simply: the belief that you won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up. It sounds simple. In practice, it's one of the rarest dynamics a team can have — because between understanding the concept and actually building it, there's a gap most leaders never see.

And the cost shows up directly in the business: people stay quiet when they should speak, problems surface too late, decisions get made on incomplete information, and performance quietly turns into compliance. Most managers believe they have psychological safety. What they actually have is silence.

I've worked with very different teams. One, coming out of a toxic environment, started calling things by their name — but the fear hadn't fully disappeared. That's the reality: progress doesn't look like it does in the books. Another team, early in its journey, experimented fearlessly. They got things wrong, learned, grew fast — because no one had taught them that mistakes were dangerous. A third team, full of experts under heavy pressure, kept avoiding vulnerability. The problem was never the technical work. It was the conversations they weren't having — because that's where the real information lives: in what doesn't get said.

Psychological safety isn't built in workshops. It doesn't come from tools, or from announcing "let's be open." It comes from a leader's ability to read the team in real time and step in exactly at the moment someone hesitates to speak. That's where everything gets decided — not in strategy, not in planning, but in that small second when someone chooses between saying it and staying quiet.

If you're not sure your people are choosing to speak, chances are they aren't. And the real question isn't whether you have psychological safety — it's what you're doing differently to build it.

### What Storming Taught Me About Leading Through Difference

I became a team leader in 2015. In my early leadership training, we talked a lot about team formation — I first heard of Tuckman's model there: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, Adjourning. For a long time, I shaped my leadership style around understanding those stages. But the stage that was hardest to manage, by far, was Storming.

I had colleagues who didn't just fail to collaborate — they didn't even want to speak to one another. Different personalities, different needs, different expectations. As a leader, it stopped being about tasks. It became about tension and emotional regulation. One-on-ones, aligning expectations, clarifying what we actually needed from each other — not to become friends, but to be able to work together — were what moved the team forward. That's really the whole point of Storming: finding a way to perform despite the differences.

I tested a lot of models trying to understand that dynamic — from Jung and Freud to Brené Brown, books on emotion and relationships, various business frameworks, and eventually coaching. The insight that stuck: we don't react to the present, we react to our past experiences.

Today, the environment has changed. It's no longer just about the interactions between people. As a leader, you're not just managing relationships anymore — you're also managing how people relate to technology. AI adds a new layer of complexity on top of something that was already hard. But one thing stays constant: human interaction. AI is a tool. Performance still comes from how people work together. A high-performing team doesn't just deliver when it can — it delivers better and faster, because the people in it know how to work together despite their differences.

### How I Became a Leader

I like the word "leader." For me, it's not just a title — it's part of who I am. I choose it because I have a deep passion for working with people, discovering their potential, and identifying the environments where they can do their best work.

The road here wasn't short. It took more than 12 years of working on myself to be able to work authentically with others. I read widely — psychology, personal development, spirituality, philosophy. I went through leadership and coaching training. I sought out people who could show me new perspectives, and learned from every challenge. I tested things, got them wrong, tried something else, and kept searching, again and again.

My first year as a team leader, and later my first year as a business manager, were probably the hardest. I wanted to quit hundreds of times. And yet, every time, the right people would show up — telling me what I had to offer was needed, that I could help others.

Every change of role, team, or company brought situations I wasn't ready for. That's when I started asking myself the essential questions: Where's my limit? Where am I, in everything I do? Who am I, in everything I do? It took months to feel like myself again — to accept that I can't be everywhere, that not everything is my responsibility, and that it's healthy to share the load and look for people who can help balance me.

Today, I choose consciously how I invest my energy. ### Why High-Performing Women Lose Confidence After Becoming Mothers

Not because they become less capable. Because the rules that used to work for them stop working. Before, performance was predictable — work harder, get more; prepare better, have control. After becoming a mother, something shifts. You don't have the same time, the same energy, the same availability to say yes to everything. But the standards stay the same. That's where the fracture happens — you start comparing yourself to a version of you that no longer exists, a version with different resources, a different context, different priorities.

I've worked with women who led teams, made important decisions, were respected in their organizations — and yet, in sessions, they'd say: "I don't feel like myself anymore." "I don't have the same clarity." "It feels like I don't trust myself." None of that was actually true. The clarity hadn't disappeared. It just didn't look the same anymore.

In matrescence, it's not just your life that changes — the way you think, feel, and decide changes too. You become more selective. You sense faster what isn't yours to carry. You have less patience for things that don't really matter. But if you try to function like before, all of that starts to look like a flaw instead of a resource. So you start doubting yourself — not because you lost something, but because you haven't yet learned to trust this new version of you.

That's really what's at stake: not going back to who you were, but understanding who you are now. Because in this new version, there's a different kind of leadership available — clearer, more grounded, less willing to compromise. But you have to give it permission to exist.

## The Rainbow of Emotions

### On Emotional States and Conscious Beginnings

Fifteen years ago, when my first child was born, I felt that something about how I behaved day to day no longer matched who I wanted to be. I'd grown up in an environment where answers came from automatic habits — I repeated what I'd been taught, without realizing what had actually triggered the reaction or which emotional state came with it.

The first step toward awareness was the moment I began noticing that I was reacting — and that those reactions weren't conscious choices, but learned patterns. I believed I was acting freely, but in reality I was responding from an inner "program," built from what I'd been taught was correct or acceptable.

Over time, I learned that our reactions are closely tied to our core emotions — fear, anger, sadness, joy, disgust, surprise — and to the thoughts that come with them. Emotions don't define us as a personality, even though sometimes we identify with them completely. They're more like internal messages, valuable signals about what's happening inside us or in our relationships with others.

Understanding the core emotions helped me recognize more clearly what I was experiencing, give myself permission to feel it, and consciously choose how I wanted to respond. Every emotion carries a message. Anger tells us a boundary has been crossed. Fear warns us something is uncertain or unknown. Sadness helps us honor what we've lost. Joy connects us to authenticity and meaning.

Understanding what you feel doesn't mean solving everything — it means being aware and gentle with yourself, without judgment, without running or rushing to "fix" the state. In that moment, you start being truly present with yourself. And that's when you can discover that your closest friend is you.

### The Rainbow Within Us

Every emotion is like a color in the rainbow — it has its own charm, its own vibration, its own unique message about who we are and what we choose to do with what we feel.

But what happens when we try to deny an emotion? It's like trying not to see one of the rainbow's colors. Imagine the effort that takes — not just the eyes have to ignore it, but the mind, the body, the felt sense too. And over time, we discover that the energy spent denying one emotion starts to dull the others as well.

That's how we stop feeling — how we stop knowing why a sunrise no longer moves us, why we feel empty in the middle of a conversation, why we react "differently" and no longer recognize ourselves in our own bodies, why our relationships start to feel cold, distant, or forced.

When was the last time we truly listened to our inner self — with its whole rainbow of emotions? And if we did, what did it tell us?

For me, every day is a return to feeling. Every emotion that shows up is an invitation to understand: what brought me to this state? What is there for me to see here? Every day, I learn — about myself, about others, about relationships. I learn how, sometimes, we get lost in reactions and emotions that no longer belong to us. Learning isn't comfortable. It turns your values, your beliefs, your sense of order upside down. Learning is about unlearning, so there's room for the new. And the new is hard — it comes with uncertainty, with insecurity, with questions we don't have immediate answers to.

It's the same with emotions. I rediscover them every day. I feel them differently than yesterday, I listen to them with a more attentive ear, I ask them what they want from me. And every time, the answer is different, but the meaning stays the same: it's about how I relate to myself, to others, to the world.

We live in a society that tends to standardize us — to demand conformity, to impose adaptation. And sometimes, in the wish to belong, we forget who we are. But today we have the means to return to our inner self. My grandmother used to tell me, whenever she gave me something: "It's not from me. It's from God. And if He gave it to me, I share it — because for me, it's too much." I've carried that lesson into every relationship I have: each of us carries something unique to offer others, and maybe it's only together that we can become whole.

### Fear Is Not the Enemy. It's a Messenger.

Fear works in three ways: fight, flight, or freeze. In front of fear, I freeze. My brain seems to stop — no clear thoughts, as if an invisible wall rises between me and the world. That mechanism, familiar as it is, kept me stuck in automatic reactions for a long time.

Because I know that about myself, I give myself time — time for the amygdala to send its signals, time for the neocortex to soothe the past and remind me: there's no danger right now. It's just an opportunity.

I don't like change, and yet I've chosen to live through it often. I changed roles at work every two years, precisely to step outside my comfort zone — and that's how I learned that fear was protecting me from an imagined danger, not a real one. I also learned something essential: everyone has their own mechanism. Some flee, some attack. Some go silent, some shout. All of them are forms of self-protection.

What matters isn't reacting "correctly" — it's knowing ourselves, understanding our reactions, and adjusting to them with gentleness. As Joe Dispenza writes in *Breaking the Habit of Being Yourself*, the brain is wired to specific emotions and chemically dependent on them — when life doesn't produce the familiar chemistry, we do everything we can to recreate it. Even the smallest changes — how we dry ourselves with a towel, how we sit at the table — can trigger discomfort. The brain seeks the familiar, not the true. That's why we return to old patterns.

But if we want something different, we have to step off autopilot. Maybe it all starts with a simple question: what am I feeling right now?

Reaction comes from the past. Action comes from the present. Reaction is conditioned. Action is chosen. Reaction controls us. Action empowers us. Transformation begins with observation — catching yourself in the middle of an emotion and taking a step back is an act of courage and emotional maturity. What am I really feeling right now? What is my brain telling me — is it real, or just familiar? If I acted instead of reacted, what would I choose?

Maybe we don't always succeed. But every moment of clarity builds a new connection within us — and that's how change begins: authentic, owned, and gentle.

### Anger: The Emotion That Says "Enough"

Anger is an intense emotion, often compared to a volcano. It shows up when we perceive an injustice, or when someone crosses a personal boundary. As a child, I wasn't allowed to be angry. In the social context I grew up in, expressing emotion loudly — especially anger — was considered unacceptable. You didn't raise your voice, you didn't talk back to an adult. So for years, my anger was put on mute.

What did that look like? I had no voice. I didn't say no. I accepted whatever came, without questioning it. Slowly but surely, I learned to self-sabotage, because somewhere inside me was the deeply rooted belief that "it's not okay to be angry." In my professional relationships, I ran from anger. Instead, I built spaces where I could understand how people function, with their whole rainbow of emotions — and that's how I came to understand that anger isn't a mistake.

Anger shows up to say stop. To signal that someone has crossed into our space. To bring an injustice into the light. I learned that you don't need to shout, but it's essential to say what you feel — to explain the context that led you there, to clarify and align the relationships where you feel your boundaries are being ignored. That understanding helped me choose consciously: which relationships to keep, which ones need to end, and what I need to protect my own space.

Anger sometimes needs to be moved — worked out through the body, through running, dancing, walking, or other physical release. Left unexamined, anger can become destructive. But listened to, expressed, and directed with intention, it can become an enormous source of clarity and action. Anger isn't our enemy. It's the guardian of our boundaries.

### Sadness: The Emotion That Asks for Time and Gentleness

Sadness never announces when it's coming. It catches me off guard, and often, I've refused to let it in. The fight with it isn't visible — it happens inside, in silence. Sometimes I avoid it by staying busy, by numbing the state. But it comes back, more intense, seemingly out of nowhere.

Over time, I learned to let it come — to make room for it, to listen to it, to hold it. Sadness needs space, not denial. It needs gentleness, not urgency. Its most familiar face is grief — but grief doesn't only show up when we lose someone we love. It's born from the end of a relationship, a team, a place we grew in, or a dream that never came true. It can even show up when we lose an object with meaning, or an animal that was our emotional support.

What does sadness do? It slows our pace, so we can process. It helps us look deeper inside, to understand what hurts, what we've lost, and what needs rebuilding. In the body, sadness feels like a weight in the chest, a lack of energy, a pressure in the throat or stomach. It's the emotion that calls us to withdraw a little, to be with ourselves, to breathe, to give ourselves permission not to be "fine" right away.

Instead of running from it, I began making room for it inside me. I started telling myself: "It's okay to feel. It's okay to cry. It's okay not to know how to move forward right now." And every time I sat with sadness, it passed — not because I chased it away, but because I honored it.

Sadness isn't random. It's an inner signal that something mattered to us — that a person, a dream, a connection, a part of our life had real meaning. Feeling the pain of loss or separation isn't a sign of weakness; it's a sign of the value that thing held for us. When we choose to listen to sadness, it can speak to us about deep needs, about personal boundaries, about meaning and belonging. It can show us what we truly want to keep, and what we're ready to let go of. In that way, sadness becomes a bridge — between what was and what can be.

### Joy: The Emotion of Connection and Coming Home

For me, joy is the emotion that fills the soul — the moment when every inner gap seems to disappear. It's the deep quiet that comes from a real connection — with a person, with nature, with myself. It's relaxation, trust, and presence.

Joy is a baby's genuine laugh. It's a child running carefree, singing. It's the moment I find myself in a silent embrace, where no words are needed to feel that we're together. Joy is "home" — that inner space where I don't have to be anything other than what I am.

I've discovered that joy is born from real connection, not from outside validation. It doesn't need performance — it needs authenticity, openness, honesty, human closeness. It needs simple moments that become precious precisely because they're unforced. In relation to nature or animals, joy shows up as pure energy — no masks, just presence.

I've truly learned what joy means since becoming a mother. One of my children taught me to enjoy the rain and see it as a blessing. Another taught me to "let it go" — to release what's no longer mine to hold. One of the books that shaped how I choose to raise my children is *Pollyanna*, with its simple, profound gift — the gift of joy. I've learned, alongside my kids, that joy doesn't need to be sought. It needs to be recognized.

For me, joy isn't a loud emotion. It's warm, deep, and gentle — the quiet of the soul, knowing you're well exactly as you are.

### Disgust: The Discomfort That Protects Us

Disgust isn't just about spoiled food or unpleasant smells. It's far more subtle, and often far more valuable than we let ourselves recognize.

For me, disgust shows up as a moment of questions: "What's wrong here?" "Why is my body reacting this way?" It's a clear signal of discomfort, and in that moment, I know it's time to pay close attention to what's happening. When it comes up in a conversation or a relationship, disgust puts me on alert — not to judge, but to investigate. Is there something in the tone that doesn't match the message? Is there a microexpression that contradicts the words? Do I feel my values are being contradicted, or ignored?

In those moments, disgust helps me identify the incongruence — the sense that something isn't aligned, either with me or with the reality of the situation. It's an instinct that, without having all the words yet, tells you something needs attention. That maybe you're being subtly manipulated, that maybe a boundary is being crossed, that maybe you're saying yes where you should be saying no.

I've learned not to ignore disgust, because behind it sit my personal boundaries and my inner truth. But disgust isn't only about human interaction — it also shows up when we're making an aesthetic judgment: do I like this scent or not? Does this image inspire me, or repel me? Does this space feel right for me, or do I want to leave?

In that sense, disgust becomes a fine filter for our authentic preferences — a barometer of our values and identity. It's a message about who we are, and what fits us. We may not talk about it often. But disgust knows us. And it guides us.

### Surprise: The Emotion That Breaks Our Rhythm and Invites a Choice

I've always experienced surprise as an emotion that freezes the mind for a moment — a sudden, brief pause, where something unexpected enters our reality and shifts our state.

Sometimes joy follows surprise right away — good news, an unplanned meeting, a tender gesture. Other times, sadness, anger, or frustration follow — because what arrived didn't match what we had in mind.

Surprise is short-lived, but its impact is instant. If I've made a plan, set a clear direction, and suddenly something unforeseen shows up — "a surprise" — my mind can interpret that event as a danger. Not because it is one, but because it's different from what I anticipated. The emotional brain reacts not to reality, but to the loss of control over prediction.

As a parent, I've learned that surprise moments are a constant — something falls, someone spills, something happens differently than planned. In those moments, we have two options: consciously choose how to live the next moment, or react automatically, using lines and gestures we once saw as children — even if we promised ourselves we wouldn't repeat them.

Surprise carries the potential for a choice within it. But for that choice to be a conscious one, we need to recognize the instant our mind wants to run, control, or react. We need to ask ourselves, even for a second: is this really a danger, or just a moment of discomfort?

Getting to a rational place when a surprise is unwelcome isn't simple — it takes years of inner work, introspection, gentleness, and practice. But it's possible. And with every conscious choice, we don't just handle surprises better — we become more present in our own lives.

### Sawubona — I See You. And for a Moment, We Exist Together.

I recently discovered a form of greeting that made me feel something deeply, unsettlingly honest: "Sawubona," the traditional Zulu greeting, which means "I see you." Not a simple "hi" or "hello" — but "I see you." A statement that recognizes the other person's presence, not just physically, but emotionally, humanly, deeply.

I found myself wondering: what would it be like to meet someone and, instead of "hi," say "I see you"? What impact would that have on them — and on us?

For me, "I see you" means far more than visual recognition. It's an opening toward the other person, an invitation to authenticity and presence. It's a way of saying, "I recognize you as human, with everything you are — emotional states, flaws, hopes." It's a declaration of unconditional acceptance, without judgment, without the need to correct or change anything. It's like an extended hand — not to shape, but to support.

And I thought about the context we live in now — a world, and a Romania, growing more polarized, where differences build walls faster than bridges. What would change if, instead of labels and automatic reactions, we started relationships with "I see you"?

What's even more beautiful is that the traditional response to this greeting is "Shiboka," which means "I exist for you." Think about the space of safety that gets created in that exchange: I see you, and you respond that you exist for me. We're not just recognizing each other — we're validating each other's presence.

What would the world look like if we began our conversations with these simple, meaningful words? How would our relationships change if we paused, for a moment, to truly see one another?

### What Stoic Philosophy Taught Me About Responding to Change

During a period full of searching, uncertainty, and change, Stoic philosophy became like an anchor for me. It helped me shift my focus from "what's happening to me" to "how do I respond to this."

Marcus Aurelius wrote that if something external upsets you, the pain isn't from the thing itself, but from how you perceive it — and that perception, you can change at any time.

But how easy is it, really, to shift your perspective? How many times have we told ourselves "I'll change tomorrow," and turned back anyway? How many times have we stopped, because it felt too hard, too uncertain, too much?

For a while now, I've wanted to create a space where these questions don't get standard answers, but are explored with gentleness — no pressure, no big promises. Just presence, and questions that open things up.

How do you respond to change?
